## vstg-0181 — Gleaning and the right of the poor

- Código visible: vstg.1788
- Concepción: 1788 · Cierre: 1788 · Distancia a 2020: 232
- [desc.] Gleaning, the collection of crops left in the field after harvest, was a legal right of the poor in many European places. In eighteenth-century England landless cottagers held it, and a village sexton rang a bell to mark when gleaning began and ended. The custom has biblical roots in Leviticus and Deuteronomy.
- [f.f. expl.] The English legal right effectively ended after the 1788 decision in Steel v Houghton. The article also says mid-nineteenth-century technology greatly reduced gleaning.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] In 2020 there were 143 gleaning organisations in the United States and Canada combined, harvesting an estimated 163,000 to 5.2 million pounds of food. The UK Gleaning Network and the US National Gleaning Project link modern gleaning and food-recovery groups, and Agnès Varda's film The Gleaners and I (2000) explored gleaning in rural France.

## vstg-0183 — Chimney sweeps and the boys who climbed

- Código visible: vstg.1875
- Concepción: 1840 · Cierre: 1875 · Distancia a 2020: 180
- [desc.] The Chimney Sweepers and Chimneys Regulation Act of 1840 made it illegal for anyone under 21 to sweep chimneys, though it was widely ignored. Lord Shaftesbury pushed the Chimney Sweepers Act through Parliament in September 1875, which finally stopped the practice of sending boys up chimneys.
- [f.f. expl.] The 1840 law was widely ignored, and the boys who climbed chimneys kept working for another thirty-five years until the 1875 ban.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Chimney sweeping remains a working trade: the CSIA and the National Chimney Sweep Guild certify sweeps in the US. In Great Britain a bride seeing a sweep on her wedding day is considered lucky, and some sweeps attend weddings for this purpose; Rochester, Kent, revived its Sweeps' Festival in 1980.

## vstg-0184 — The red telephone box

- Código visible: vstg.1924.b
- Concepción: 1924 · Cierre: n/d · Distancia a 2020: 96
- [desc.] Sir Giles Gilbert Scott's design won a 1924 competition and became the K2, which went into service in 1926; his K6 followed in 1935. Phone boxes peaked at about 100,000 in 1990.
- [f.f. expl.] The public payphone as a fixture of every street. In 1984 BT introduced the KX100 to replace most existing boxes, and payphone use fell from about 800 million minutes in 2002 to 7 million by 2020.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] About 8,000 traditional red boxes remained in public service in January 2020. A K6 in Westbury-sub-Mendip became a library in 2009, a box in Glendaruel, Argyll, was fitted with a defibrillator after a Girl Guide competition in 2011, and the K2 prototype at Burlington House was listed Grade II* in 2019.

## vstg-0185 — The matchgirls' strike, Bryant & May

- Código visible: vstg.1888
- Concepción: 1888 · Cierre: 1888 · Distancia a 2020: 132
- [desc.] In July 1888 women and girls at Bryant & May's match factory in Bow, London, walked out over unfair fines, deductions from pay and dangerous conditions. About 1,400 had refused to work by the end of the first day.
- [f.f. expl.] The strike ended on 16 July 1888 with the abolition of unfair fines and deductions and with direct access to management. The success led to the Union of Women Matchmakers, formed later that year.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Bryant & May announced in 1901 that its factory no longer used white phosphorus, and the White Phosphorus Matches Prohibition Act 1908 barred its use in the UK after 31 December 1910. The strike was marked on its 125th anniversary in London in 2013, featured in a BBC Ripper Street episode that November, and in a 2017 U2 tour tribute.

## vstg-0186 — Blockbuster, the video rental chain

- Código visible: vstg.1985
- Concepción: 1985 · Cierre: 2013 · Distancia a 2020: 35
- [desc.] David Cook opened the first Blockbuster in Dallas, Texas, on 19 October 1985. By 2004 it had more than 9,000 stores and 84,300 employees.
- [f.f. expl.] A chain meant to remain the way people rent films. In August 2020 the Bend location was listed as an Airbnb rental for a 1990s-themed sleepover.

## vstg-0188 — Organ grinder and the barrel organ

- Código visible: vstg.1935.b
- Concepción: 1935 · Cierre: 1935 · Distancia a 2020: 85
- [desc.] Organ grinders with barrel organs were common street performers in Europe and New York in the nineteenth century. By 1880 nearly one in twenty Italian men in certain areas were organ grinders.
- [f.f. expl.] Organ grinders largely disappeared from European streets in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In New York, Mayor Fiorello La Guardia banned them from the streets in 1935, citing traffic, begging and organised crime's role in renting out the machines.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Dutch street organs were common until the start of the 21st century and are on display at the Museum Speelklok in Utrecht and the Draaiorgelmuseum in Haarlem. Organ rallies such as MEMUSI in Vienna gather enthusiasts in period costume, and organ grinders are common in Mexico City and in Bilbao, Vitoria and San Sebastián. New York's 1935 ban was repealed in 1975.

## vstg-0189 — Hansom cab, from patent to last licence

- Código visible: vstg.1947
- Concepción: 1834 · Cierre: 1947 · Distancia a 2020: 186
- [desc.] Joseph Hansom patented the hansom cab in 1834. It became the most common vehicle for hire in the nineteenth century and was exported to Europe, other British colonies and the United States.
- [f.f. expl.] Horse-drawn cabs were superseded by motor vehicles. Petrol-powered taximeter cars were introduced in 1908, and by the early 1920s horse-drawn cabs had largely been superseded. The last licence for a horse-drawn cab in London was relinquished in 1947.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] The Sherlock Holmes Museum in London owns and operates a surviving hansom cab; restored cabs are held by the Remington Carriage Museum in Alberta, the Smithsonian National Museum of American History and Hinckley and Bosworth Borough Council. Production companies still rent replicas or restored originals as props.

## vstg-0190 — Lamplighter and the gas street lamp

- Código visible: vstg.1957
- Concepción: 1807 · Cierre: 1957 · Distancia a 2020: 213
- [desc.] Lamplighters lit and extinguished the gas street lamps of cities. Gas lighting took over from candles and oil lamps after Frederick Albert Winsor's 1807 exhibition. Baltimore removed nearly all of its gas streetlights in 1957.
- [f.f. expl.] The trade ended as gas gave way to electric light and automatic systems reduced the need for lamplighters. Baltimore removed nearly all of its gas streetlights in 1957.

About 1,500 gas lamps still operate in central London, Berlin has about 37,000 (as of 2014), and Boston has more than 2,800 in its historic districts.

## vstg-0191 — The rag-and-bone man

- Código visible: vstg.1965
- Concepción: 1965 · Cierre: 1965 · Distancia a 2020: 55
- [desc.] The rag-and-bone man collected rags, bones, metal and paper from households and sold them to merchants, usually on foot or with a horse-drawn cart. Henry Mayhew's 1851 report counted 800 to 1,000 'bone-grubbers and rag-gatherers' in London alone.
- [f.f. expl.] By the mid-1960s the trade had fallen into decline. A 1965 report found only a few hundred rag-and-bone men left in London, and in Manchester and Salford about sixty rag merchants in the 1950s had dropped to about twelve by 1978.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Rag-and-bone collection continues in the 21st century: rising scrap prices brought rag-and-bone men back in the UK, now in vans with megaphones (a 2011 Daily Telegraph story reported complaints about the noise). In India, a 2017 study reported by the Hindustan Times put ragpicking at about ₹3,200 crore and credited it with the country's near-90% recycling rate for PET bottles; in 2015 the Environment Minister created a national award for ragpickers.

## vstg-0192 — The home milk round

- Código visible: vstg.1975.c
- Concepción: 1975 · Cierre: 1975 · Distancia a 2020: 45
- [desc.] Home milk delivery was common from the late nineteenth century through the twentieth century. In Britain milkmen were delivering milk in bottles by 1880.
- [f.f. expl.] The decline came in the second half of the twentieth century. In the United States, the share of consumers with milk delivered fell from about 29.7% in 1963 to 6.9% of total sales by 1975, as supermarkets and household refrigerators let people buy milk whenever they wanted.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] By 2005 about 0.4% of US consumers had milk delivered, and a handful of companies had sprung up to offer the service; interest kept growing in the 2010s as part of the local food movement. During the coronavirus outbreak in 2020 some remaining milkmen saw demand jump.

- [desc.] The term 'penny arcade' came into use around 1905–1910. The video arcade boom of the early 1980s more than doubled North American video arcades between 1980 and 1982, to about 13,000.

## vstg-0195 — Morse code in maritime distress

- Código visible: vstg.1999.b
- Concepción: 1999 · Cierre: 1999 · Distancia a 2020: 21
- [desc.] Morse code was the international standard for maritime distress until 1999. The French Navy stopped using it on 31 January 1997.
- [f.f. expl.] Satellite and VHF systems replaced Morse with the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS). The United States Coast Guard has ceased all use of Morse code on the radio.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Morse remains most popular among amateur radio operators; the FCC removed all Morse proficiency requirements on 23 February 2007. VOR and NDB radio navigation aids still broadcast identifiers in Morse, and Android 5.0 and later let users enter text in Morse as an assistive input.

- [desc.] Knocker-uppers were paid to wake sleeping workers so they could reach work on time. They tapped on windows with a baton or a long, light stick, often bamboo. The role began during the Industrial Revolution in Britain, the Netherlands and Ireland.

## vstg-0198 — Night soil and the gong farmers

- Código visible: vstg.None
- Concepción: n/d · Cierre: n/d · Distancia a 2020: n/d
- [desc.] Night soil collectors removed excreta from privies and cesspits. In Tudor England they were called 'gong farmers'; later they were called 'night soil men' or 'nightmen'. Rapid urbanisation in the nineteenth century led to outhouse-based collection systems such as the Rochdale system.
- [f.f. expl.] Sewerage replaced the system. The article says most inner-city areas in Australia were connected to the sewer in the early 1900s, and night soil fertiliser use fell sharply after the Second World War.
- [Efecto en el presente — documented] Night soil collection continues in some areas of Japan (kumitori benjo toilets). Manual scavenging was reported in 2014 in Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan despite India's 1993 law banning it, and night soil is used as fertiliser in North Korea.

## vstg-0199 — The blacksmith and the automobile

- Código visible: vstg.None
- Concepción: n/d · Cierre: n/d · Distancia a 2020: n/d
- [desc.] Blacksmiths forged wrought iron and steel into tools, farm implements and utensils. As demand for their products fell, many earned money shoeing horses; by the 1960s most working blacksmiths in the United States were doing farrier work.
- [f.f. expl.] The number of blacksmiths kept decreasing with the arrival of automobiles, and many former blacksmiths became some of the first car mechanics. The low point for blacksmithing in the United States was reached in the 1960s.

Hefaiston in the Czech Republic is a major annual meeting of artist-blacksmiths.
