## vstg-0181 — Gleaning and the right of the poor

**Código visible:** vstg.1788  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: derecho de espigar para los cottagers; campana del sacristán; fin efectivo con Steel v Houghton (1788).

**[desc.]** Gleaning, the collection of crops left in the field after harvest, was a legal right of the poor in many European places. In eighteenth-century England landless cottagers held it, and a village sexton rang a bell to mark when gleaning began and ended. The custom has biblical roots in Leviticus and Deuteronomy.

**[f.f. expl.]** The English legal right effectively ended after the 1788 decision in Steel v Houghton. The article also says mid-nineteenth-century technology greatly reduced gleaning.

## vstg-0183 — Chimney sweeps and the boys who climbed

**Código visible:** vstg.1875  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: ley de 1840 'ampliamente ignorada'; ley de 1875 aprobada en septiembre de 1875.

**[desc.]** The Chimney Sweepers and Chimneys Regulation Act of 1840 made it illegal for anyone under 21 to sweep chimneys, though it was widely ignored. Lord Shaftesbury pushed the Chimney Sweepers Act through Parliament in September 1875, which finally stopped the practice of sending boys up chimneys.

**[f.f. expl.]** The 1840 law was widely ignored, and the boys who climbed chimneys kept working for another thirty-five years until the 1875 ban.

## vstg-0184 — The first manual telephone switchboard

## vstg-0186 — Natural ice, from Tudor to the refrigerator

**Código visible:** vstg.1914  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: inicio en 1806 con Tudor; plantas superan la cosecha natural en 1914; colapso tras la guerra.

**[desc.]** Frederic Tudor began shipping natural ice in 1806, when he sent it to Martinique. The natural ice trade peaked at the end of the nineteenth century.

**[f.f. expl.]** By 1914 American plants were producing more ice than was harvested naturally. After the First World War the natural ice industry collapsed, and cheap electric motors made home refrigerators common in American homes by the 1930s.

## vstg-0188 — Organ grinder and the barrel organ

**[desc.]** Organ grinders with barrel organs were common street performers in Europe and New York in the nineteenth century. By 1880 nearly one in twenty Italian men in certain areas were organ grinders.

**[f.f. expl.]** Organ grinders largely disappeared from European streets in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In New York, Mayor Fiorello La Guardia banned them from the streets in 1935, citing traffic, begging and organised crime's role in renting out the machines.

## vstg-0189 — Hansom cab, from patent to last licence

**Código visible:** vstg.1947  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: patente de 1834; taxímetros de gasolina en 1908; última licencia de coche de caballos en Londres en 1947.

**[desc.]** Joseph Hansom patented the hansom cab in 1834. It became the most common vehicle for hire in the nineteenth century and was exported to Europe, other British colonies and the United States.

**[f.f. expl.]** Horse-drawn cabs were superseded by motor vehicles. Petrol-powered taximeter cars were introduced in 1908, and by the early 1920s horse-drawn cabs had largely been superseded. The last licence for a horse-drawn cab in London was relinquished in 1947.

## vstg-0191 — The rag-and-bone man

**Código visible:** vstg.1965  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: Mayhew (1851); informe de 1965 con 'unos pocos cientos'; Manchester y Salford.

**[desc.]** The rag-and-bone man collected rags, bones, metal and paper from households and sold them to merchants, usually on foot or with a horse-drawn cart. Henry Mayhew's 1851 report counted 800 to 1,000 'bone-grubbers and rag-gatherers' in London alone.

**[f.f. expl.]** By the mid-1960s the trade had fallen into decline. A 1965 report found only a few hundred rag-and-bone men left in London, and in Manchester and Salford about sixty rag merchants in the 1950s had dropped to about twelve by 1978.

## vstg-0196 — Knocker-up, the human alarm clock

**Código visible:** vstg.NA  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: origen en la Revolución Industrial; declive en los años cuarenta y cincuenta; pervivencia hasta comienzos de los setenta en algunas zonas.

**[desc.]** Knocker-uppers were paid to wake sleeping workers so they could reach work on time. They tapped on windows with a baton or a long, light stick, often bamboo. The role began during the Industrial Revolution in Britain, the Netherlands and Ireland.

**[f.f. expl.]** The job had more or less died out by the 1940s and 1950s, but it persisted in some parts of industrial England until the early 1970s.

## vstg-0198 — Night soil and the gong farmers

**Código visible:** vstg.NA  ·  **Nivel:** documented  ·  **Estado:** Verificada con Wikipedia: 'gong farmers' en la Inglaterra Tudor; sistema de Rochdale; conexión al alcantarillado en Australia a comienzos del siglo XX.

**[desc.]** Night soil collectors removed excreta from privies and cesspits. In Tudor England they were called 'gong farmers'; later they were called 'night soil men' or 'nightmen'. Rapid urbanisation in the nineteenth century led to outhouse-based collection systems such as the Rochdale system.

**[f.f. expl.]** Sewerage replaced the system. The article says most inner-city areas in Australia were connected to the sewer in the early 1900s, and night soil fertiliser use fell sharply after the Second World War.

## vstg-0199 — The blacksmith and the automobile

**[desc.]** Blacksmiths forged wrought iron and steel into tools, farm implements and utensils. As demand for their products fell, many earned money shoeing horses; by the 1960s most working blacksmiths in the United States were doing farrier work.

**[f.f. expl.]** The number of blacksmiths kept decreasing with the arrival of automobiles, and many former blacksmiths became some of the first car mechanics. The low point for blacksmithing in the United States was reached in the 1960s.
